# Political Economy Analysis

**ANBU Division 008 · Analytics**

---

## Purpose

Political economy analysis examines how economic structures, resource distribution, and material interests shape political outcomes — and how political power shapes economic arrangements. Politics and economics are not separate spheres; budgets, property rights, trade, and finance are exercised through political institutions, and political coalitions are sustained by economic interests. This file provides the framework for analyzing these interactions from public data.

## Scope

Applied to questions where economic and political factors interact: policy adoption, coalition stability, state capacity, development outcomes, sanctions effects, institutional reform, and any assessment where "who benefits economically" explains political behavior.

## Intelligence objectives

- Identify the economic interests that sustain or threaten political arrangements.
- Analyze distributional effects of policies: who gains, who loses, who is compensated.
- Assess state economic capacity: revenue, control of assets, fiscal space.
- Predict political responses to economic shocks and reform attempts.

## Core concepts

- **Distributional politics.** Policies create winners and losers; losers organize to block, winners to defend.
- **Fiscal contract.** The implicit bargain: citizens pay taxes, state provides services and security; breakdown produces crisis.
- **Rent-seeking.** Actors using political power to extract economic rents rather than create value — often the engine of state capture.
- **State economic capacity.** The state's ability to tax, borrow, control assets, and deliver — distinct from political authority.
- **Coalition economics.** Ruling coalitions are held together by distributing economic benefits; analysis of who gets what explains stability.
- **Structural adjustment legacy.** Historical economic choices constrain present options; path dependence matters.
- **Informal economy.** Economic activity outside formal regulation shapes both state capacity and political behavior.

## Analytical methodology

1. Identify the economic dimension of the question: budgets, property, trade, finance, distribution.
2. Map economic actors and their interests via `stakeholder_analysis.md`.
3. Analyze the distributional effects of the policy or development in question.
4. Assess state economic capacity: revenue sources, fiscal space, asset control.
5. Identify rent-seeking channels and capture risks via `influence_network_assessment.md`.
6. Examine coalition economics: who benefits from the current arrangement?
7. Project political responses: who blocks, who mobilizes, what compromises emerge?
8. Report with economic evidence cited and political implications stated.

## Step-by-step workflow

1. Define the political question and its economic dimension.
2. Pull budget, revenue, and expenditure data for the relevant period.
3. Identify the distributional incidence of the policy/development.
4. Map economic stakeholders and their political leverage.
5. Assess state capacity: tax collection, control of SOEs, monetary/fiscal tools.
6. Identify rent-seeking and capture channels.
7. Analyze coalition maintenance: what economic benefits hold the arrangement together?
8. Write the political-economy assessment with evidence and implications.

## Data requirements

- Budget laws, appropriation documents, revenue statistics.
- Central bank publications: monetary policy, financial stability, balance of payments.
- SOE financial reports and ownership registers.
- Trade statistics and tariff schedules.
- Property and land records where public.
- Tax expenditure and subsidy reports.
- Household/business surveys for distributional analysis.
- Historical fiscal data for path-dependence analysis.

## Approved public sources

Finance ministries and budget offices; central banks; national statistics offices; revenue authorities; SOE annual reports; trade ministries and customs data; multilateral institutions (IMF Article IV reports, World Bank public diagnostics); parliamentary budget records; audit institution reports.

## Source verification standards

Economic data must come from official statistics or audited sources. Political claims about economic motives must cite observable evidence (budget allocations, appointments, votes) not speculation about private interests. IMF/WB reports are analytical products themselves — use their data, scrutinize their conclusions.

## Analytical framework

Political-economy questions checklist:

| Question | Data source |
|---|---|
| Who gains/loses economically? | Budget incidence, household surveys |
| What is the fiscal cost? | Budget documents, debt statistics |
| Who controls economic assets? | Ownership registers, SOE reports |
| What rent-seeking channels exist? | Procurement, licensing, regulation |
| What binds the ruling coalition? | Distribution of benefits, appointments |
| What is the state's economic capacity? | Revenue, borrowing, control instruments |

## Evidence requirements

- Economic claims cite official statistics or audited sources.
- Distributional claims cite incidence data or documented allocations.
- Rent-seeking claims cite procurement records, licensing patterns, or documented conflicts.
- Coalition analysis cites who receives what benefits.
- Political implications drawn explicitly, not assumed.

## Confidence assessment

- **HIGH** — economic data comprehensive; distributional effects documented; political implications follow directly.
- **MODERATE** — economic data solid; political implications plausible but dependent on unobserved bargaining.
- **LOW** — economic data patchy; distributional analysis incomplete.
- **INSUFFICIENT** — economic data unavailable or unreliable; report observability gap.

## Common analytical mistakes

- Treating economics as separate from politics ("just economics" ignores distributional conflict).
- Ignoring informal economy and off-budget activity.
- Assuming stated policy goals match actual distributional effects.
- Missing rent-seeking because it is hidden in "administrative" decisions.
- Confusing state economic capacity with state political authority.
- Applying developed-country fiscal models to contexts with different structures.

## Ethical considerations

Political-economy analysis reveals who benefits from arrangements — legitimate accountability work. It must not be used to advocate for or against specific economic systems ideologically, and must not target individuals for their lawful economic activity.

## Security boundaries

Public economic and political data only. No claims about private wealth, hidden assets, or secret financial dealings without public evidence. No economic analysis framed as targeting dissidents or opponents.

## Expected output

Political-economy annex: economic data summary, distributional analysis, rent-seeking assessment, coalition economics, political implications. Main product integrates economic drivers into the assessment.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Economic data from official/audited sources.
- [ ] Distributional effects analyzed, not just aggregate.
- [ ] Rent-seeking channels identified.
- [ ] Coalition economics explained.
- [ ] State capacity assessed separately from authority.
- [ ] Political implications explicit and evidenced.
