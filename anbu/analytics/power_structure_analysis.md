# Power Structure Analysis

**ANBU Division 008 · Analytics**

---

## Purpose

Formal institutions describe who is authorized to decide; power structure analysis describes who actually decides. Power in states and organizations rarely matches the org chart — it concentrates in patronage networks, control of security forces, gatekeeping positions, and control of information flows. This file defines how ANBU analysts assess where effective power sits, how it is exercised, and how it may shift.

## Scope

Applied to states (regime power structures, civil-military relations, ruling coalitions), organizations (who controls budgets and appointments), parties (factions and machines), and any context where formal authority and actual power diverge. Individual-level power profiling of private persons is out of scope.

## Intelligence objectives

- Identify where effective power concentrates beyond formal titles.
- Map the bases of power: coercive, economic, institutional, charismatic, informational.
- Detect power shifts: rising and declining actors before they become formal facts.
- Assess regime or institutional stability through power-structure lens.

## Core concepts

- **Formal vs. effective power.** Authority on paper vs. ability to get outcomes. The gap between them is the analytical object.
- **Bases of power.** French-Raven typology adapted: coercive (force), reward (resources), legitimate (office), expert (knowledge), referent (charisma/identity).
- **Principal power node.** The actor(s) whose support is necessary for major decisions — not necessarily the titular head.
- **Veto power.** Ability to block without ability to initiate.
- **Power broker.** Actor who controls access between otherwise disconnected power nodes.
- **Coalition maintenance.** Rulers stay in power by keeping winning coalitions satisfied; analyzing the coalition explains behavior better than analyzing the leader.
- **Shadow structures.** Parallel decision channels (security services, party machines, family networks) that may supersede formal ones.

## Analytical methodology

1. Map formal authority structures from official sources.
2. Identify indicators of effective power: who controls appointments, budgets, security, information.
3. Assess power bases for each key actor.
4. Determine the principal power node(s) and veto players.
5. Map the ruling/decision-making coalition and its maintenance requirements.
6. Look for shadow structures and parallel chains of command.
7. Assess power balance: concentrated vs. distributed, stable vs. contested.
8. Report with evidence for each power claim — power assertions require observable indicators.

## Step-by-step workflow

1. Define the power question: who decides X, who can block X, who benefits.
2. Build formal authority map via `institutional_relationship_mapping.md`.
3. Collect power indicators: appointment patterns, budget control, security command, media control, succession outcomes.
4. For each key actor, assess bases of power and dependencies.
5. Identify the minimal winning coalition for key decisions.
6. Probe for shadow structures: parallel security chains, party machines, family/business networks documented publicly.
7. Evaluate balance: is power concentrated, shared, or contested? What are the stress points?
8. Write power-structure assessment with evidence per claim and confidence.

## Data requirements

- Official appointment and dismissal records (revealing who appoints whom).
- Budget and resource allocation documents.
- Military/security command structures and public indicators of loyalty.
- Succession events and their outcomes as power evidence.
- Public statements, leaks-adjacent official disclosures, court cases revealing internal conflict.
- Historical patterns of power exercise in the polity.

## Approved public sources

Official appointment decrees and government directories; defense and security establishment publications; budget documents; parliamentary records; court cases involving officials; official biographies; investigative journalism based on documents (as leads); academic and think-tank analysis of civil-military relations and elite politics.

## Source verification standards

Power is inferred from observable indicators — appointments, budgets, commands, outcomes — not from rumor or speculation. Each power claim must cite the indicator. Defections and palace struggles reported by single sources are flagged as MODERATE or LOW confidence. See `../verification/elite_source_verification.md`.

## Analytical framework

Power indicators checklist:

| Indicator | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| Appointment control | Who selects key officials |
| Budget authority | Who allocates resources |
| Security command | Who controls coercion |
| Succession influence | Who decides transitions |
| Information control | Who shapes what is known |
| Veto exercise | Who has blocked and been obeyed |

## Evidence requirements

- Every power claim cites observable indicators.
- Formal vs. effective distinction maintained throughout.
- Coalition analysis shows who must be satisfied.
- Shadow-structure claims carry higher evidence burden.
- Confidence reflects indicator quality.

## Confidence assessment

- **HIGH** — power structure clear from multiple independent indicators; formal and effective power mapped consistently.
- **MODERATE** — main power nodes identified; some relationships inferred.
- **LOW** — indicators ambiguous; competing power maps plausible.
- **INSUFFICIENT** — opaque system with few public indicators; report what formal structure shows and the observability gap.

## Common analytical mistakes

- Reading titles as power (the minister may serve the party boss).
- Assuming formal chain of command equals real command (shadow chains exist).
- Ignoring the winning-coalition logic that explains seemingly irrational behavior.
- Single-factor analysis: power is multi-base; one indicator insufficient.
- Mirror-imaging: assuming power works as it would in the analyst's own system.
- Confusing access (proximity to the leader) with power (ability to decide).

## Ethical considerations

Power analysis of public institutions and officials is legitimate. It must not become a targeting dossier on individuals — describe structures and offices. No predictions of violence or instability framed as incitement. See `../security/ethical_boundaries.md`.

## Security boundaries

Public indicators only. No claims about secret police activities, covert networks, or private dealings presented as fact without public evidence. No mapping of private citizens' power or influence.

## Expected output

Power-structure annex: formal vs. effective power map, bases-of-power table, coalition analysis, veto players, stress points, confidence per claim. Main product draws conclusions about stability, decision-making, and likely behavior.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Formal and effective power distinguished throughout.
- [ ] Every power claim cites observable indicators.
- [ ] Bases of power assessed per actor.
- [ ] Coalition requirements identified.
- [ ] Shadow structures probed with evidence standard.
- [ ] Confidence calibrated to indicator quality.
