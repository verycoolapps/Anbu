# Land Force Capability Overview

*ANBU OSINT — Military & Defense Intelligence Module*

---

## Purpose

This module provides structured open-source intelligence (OSINT) tradecraft for analyzing armies, armored formations, artillery, infantry. It is designed for analysts, researchers, journalists, and policymakers who need rigorous, transparent, and legally compliant assessment of defense establishments, military capabilities, procurement, alliances, and conflict dynamics. All findings derive exclusively from publicly available information; no classified sources, covert collection, or operational targeting support are provided.

*Domain focus:* armies, armored formations, artillery, infantry.

*Key analytical notes:* Army organization charts, equipment inventories, training exercise reports, procurement of armored vehicles/artillery, IISS land forces tables.

## Scope

In scope: defense policy documents, official budgets and procurement records, public order-of-battle data, arms transfer databases, alliance structures, doctrine publications, military history, training and exercise reporting, civil-military relations, and capability assessment methodology. Out of scope: real-time operational intelligence, targeting recommendations, exploitation of unit/personnel vulnerabilities, infiltration routes, and any activity intended to cause operational harm to armed forces or civilians.


**Indonesia module** — Indonesia: TNI-AD — 8+ Kodam territorial commands, armored brigades with Leopard 2/Anoa/Cacadilla vehicles, artillery (M109, CAESAR on order), air defense battalions, Kopassus special forces.

## Intelligence objectives

Primary objectives:

1) Map land force organization. 2) Inventory armored and artillery assets. 3) Assess mobility and logistics capability. 4) Note readiness and modernization status.

## Core concepts

Open-source military intelligence relies on triangulating official statements, budget documents, procurement records, imagery, and third-party databases. Key concepts include: (1) stated vs resourced policy gap; (2) order-of-battle vs actual readiness; (3) procurement pipeline as modernization signal; (4) alliance commitments vs national capacity; (5) historical patterns as explanatory variables; (6) transparency gradients across states.

## Analytical methodology

Methodology: (1) Source triage — prefer primary official documents, then reputable secondary analysis, then crowdsourced data. (2) Temporal anchoring — record publication dates and event dates separately. (3) Cross-domain correlation — budget lines vs procurement vs imagery vs doctrine. (4) Confidence grading — assign HIGH/MODERATE/LOW/INSUFFICIENT per finding. (5) Adversarial review — actively seek disconfirming evidence.

Analytical framework: Land power assessment: brigade/armored division structure, main battle tanks, artillery/rocket systems, infantry fighting vehicles, air defense, logistics and mobility assets.

## Step-by-step workflow

1. Define the intelligence question and time horizon. 2. Collect primary sources (official documents, budgets, legislation). 3. Collect secondary sources (think tanks, academic studies, reputable journalism). 4. Build a master data table with source citations. 5. Apply the analytical framework to identify patterns and gaps. 6. Assign confidence levels per finding. 7. Document assumptions and limitations. 8. Produce structured output with citations. 9. Peer review for source quality and logical consistency. 10. Archive sources for reproducibility.

## Data requirements

Minimum dataset: (a) official policy/strategy documents; (b) 5-year budget or expenditure data; (c) major procurement programs; (d) force structure summaries from IISS/SIPRI or national equivalents; (e) alliance/defense cooperation agreements; (f) relevant conflict/exercise history; (g) key personnel or institutional changes. Data should be stored with source URL, publication date, and access date.

## Approved public sources

- Army official publications
- Equipment inventory reports
- Exercise and training reports
- IISS/SIPRI land forces tables

## Source verification standards

Verification hierarchy: (1) official government/military publications; (2) international organizations (UN, NATO, EU, AU); (3) established research institutions (SIPRI, IISS, RAND, CSIS); (4) reputable journalism with editorial standards; (5) academic peer-reviewed literature; (6) crowdsourced/open data (OSINT communities, satellite imagery analysts) — always cross-verified. Reject: anonymous blogs, social media claims without corroboration, state propaganda outlets without independent confirmation, and any source with clear financial or political conflict of interest.

## Analytical framework

Land power assessment: brigade/armored division structure, main battle tanks, artillery/rocket systems, infantry fighting vehicles, air defense, logistics and mobility assets.

Additional checks: (1) Does the finding rely on a single source? (2) Are there plausible alternative explanations? (3) Is the data current (within 24 months for budgets, 12 months for exercises)? (4) Are units and definitions consistent across sources? (5) Have we accounted for classification-driven gaps in public data?

## Evidence requirements

Each analytical claim must cite at least one primary source or two independent secondary sources. Quantitative claims require the underlying dataset or a reproducible calculation. Qualitative claims require direct quotes or paraphrases with page/section references. All sources must be publicly accessible at time of analysis; note any paywalled or restricted-access sources used.

## Confidence assessment

**HIGH**: Multiple independent primary sources, recent data, clear causal chain, no plausible alternative explanation.

**MODERATE**: Two or more credible sources, some data gaps, minor ambiguity in interpretation.

**LOW**: Single source or conflicting sources, significant data gaps, high interpretive uncertainty.

**INSUFFICIENT**: No reliable public data, heavy reliance on inference, or sources fail verification standards. In such cases, state explicitly what additional collection would be needed.

## Common analytical mistakes

1. Conflating announced plans with funded programs. 2. Treating order-of-battle numbers as combat power without readiness/sustainment context. 3. Over-weighting recent events (recency bias). 4. Ignoring classification-driven opacity — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. 5. Applying Western doctrinal assumptions to non-Western forces. 6. Failing to distinguish between procurement orders and actual deliveries. 7. Neglecting industrial capacity and maintenance as limiting factors. 8. Anchoring on a single narrative or source.

## Ethical considerations

All analysis must be based on public information and must not facilitate harm to individuals or groups. Do not profile private citizens, publish sensitive personal data, or provide information that could be used for targeting, harassment, or operational harm. Respect privacy rights, data protection laws (GDPR, local equivalents), and journalistic ethics. When analyzing conflict, prioritize civilian protection framing and avoid dehumanizing language. Clearly distinguish between verified facts and analytical judgments.

## Security boundaries

This module is strictly limited to open-source public information. It does NOT provide: targeting recommendations, operational military harm facilitation, infiltration routes, vulnerability exploitation of specific units or personnel, real-time attack or movement information, or any capability intended to assist offensive operations. Analysts must refuse requests that cross these boundaries and escalate to legal review when uncertain. All outputs are for legitimate research, journalism, policy analysis, and education.

## Expected output

Deliverables: (1) Executive summary (1-2 pages) with key findings and confidence levels. (2) Detailed analytical report with citations and data tables. (3) Source list with verification status. (4) Methodology appendix documenting collection and analysis steps. (5) Gaps and collection requirements for future work. Format follows ANBU reporting standards; see reporting/intelligence_reporting.md.
