# Critical Mineral Geopolitics Analysis
**Division:** Geopolitical | **Type:** Analytical Reference | **Version:** 1.0
## 1. Purpose

- Analyze the geopolitics of critical minerals — lithium, cobalt, rare earths, nickel, graphite — covering supply concentration, processing bottleneues, and strategic competition.
## 2. Scope

- Critical minerals for batteries, electronics, defense, and energy transition. Broad commodity markets are covered under trade_route_analysis.md.
## 3. Intelligence objectives

- Map global reserves, production, and processing capacity for critical minerals.
- Identify supply concentration risks and single points of failure.
- Analyze processing bottlenecks: refining, separation, magnet manufacturing.
- Assess strategic competition and policy responses (subsidies, stockpiles, alliances).
## 4. Core concepts

- Supply concentration and chokepoints
- Processing vs mining asymmetry
- Strategic stockpiling
- Friend-shoring and industrial policy
## 5. Analytical methodology

- Mineral-supply-chain framework: map mine→process→manufacture stages, identify concentration at each stage, assess substitutability and demand growth, and evaluate policy responses.
## 6. Step-by-step workflow

- Define the mineral scope and timeframe.
- Collect reserve/production/processing data from USGS and IEA.
- Map supply chains: mining, refining, component manufacturing.
- Assess concentration risk at each stage; identify chokepoints.
- Analyze demand drivers: EVs, defense, renewables, AI.
- Evaluate policy responses: stockpiles, subsidies, trade restrictions.
## 7. Data requirements

- USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries
- IEA critical minerals reports
- UN Comtrade mineral trade data
- National geological surveys
- Think-tank mineral studies (CSIS, RAND)
- Company disclosures and industry reports
## 8. Approved public sources

- USGS
- IEA

- National geological surveys
- CSIS
- RAND
- Industry reports (benchmark minerals intelligence)
## 9. Source verification standards

- Reserve data is probabilistic; production data is observed — distinguish. Processing capacity is often opaque;
mark estimates as such. Note that 'critical' designation varies by country.
## 10. Analytical framework

- Mineral-supply-chain framework: map mine→process→manufacture stages, identify concentration at each stage, assess substitutability and demand growth, and evaluate policy responses.
## 11. Evidence requirements

- Use the ANBU seven-label evidence vocabulary (FACT, VERIFIED, UNVERIFIED, ASSESSMENT, INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS,
SPECULATION). Every claim must carry an evidence label. Load-bearing claims require corroboration. Inferences
must be logically derivable from cited evidence. Speculation must be explicitly labeled and confined to
clearly marked sections.
## 12. Confidence assessment

- Assess confidence on three dimensions: source confidence (trustworthiness of origin), information confidence
(reliability of the specific item), and analytical confidence (soundness of the inference). Report all three.
Use HIGH (multiple corroborated sources, strong analytical basis), MODERATE (single reliable source or limited
corroboration), LOW (weak sourcing or significant analytical uncertainty), INSUFFICIENT (inadequate evidence
to support assessment).
## 13. Common analytical mistakes

- Confusing reserves with production capacity.
- Ignoring processing concentration (often higher than mining concentration).
- Treating substitution as impossible rather than slow and costly.
- Missing recycling and circular-economy responses.
## 14. Ethical considerations

- Analysis only; no assistance in mineral sanctions evasion or supply-chain sabotage.
## 15. Security boundaries

- Public sources only. Do not facilitate illegal mining, sanctions evasion, or sabotage of mineral
infrastructure.
## 16. Expected output

- A mineral-geopolitics brief: supply-chain map, concentration risks, demand outlook, policy responses,
confidence labels.

- Concentration assessed
