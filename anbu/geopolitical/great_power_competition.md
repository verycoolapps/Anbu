# Great Power Competition Analysis
**Division:** Geopolitical | **Type:** Analytical Reference | **Version:** 1.0
## 1. Purpose

- Analyze great-power competition — its domains, dynamics, and implications — across military, economic, technological, and institutional arenas.
## 2. Scope

- Great-power rivalry: primarily US–China, with Russia as a secondary actor — covering military, economic, technological, and institutional competition. Regional rivalries are covered separately.
## 3. Intelligence objectives

- Map the domains and dynamics of great-power competition.
- Analyze military competition: posture, modernization, doctrine.
- Assess economic competition: trade, investment, supply chains.
- Evaluate technological competition: AI, semiconductors, critical tech.
## 4. Core concepts

- Great-power rivalry
- Domain-specific competition
- Thucydides trap
- Institutional competition
## 5. Analytical methodology

- Great-power-framework: map competition domains, analyze military/economic/technological dynamics, assess institutional competition, and forecast trajectories and escalation risks.
## 6. Step-by-step workflow

- Define the great-power dyads and timeframe.
- Map competition domains: military, economic, technological, institutional.
- Analyze military competition: force posture, modernization, exercises, doctrine.
- Assess economic competition: trade, investment, supply chains, sanctions.
- Evaluate technological competition: AI, semiconductors, quantum, biotech.
- Analyze institutional competition: UN reform, Bretton Woods, BRICS, SCO.
## 7. Data requirements

- SIPRI and IISS military assessments
- CSIS, RAND, and Brookings great-power studies
- World Bank and IMF economic data
- UN Comtrade and trade data
- Think-tank technological competition analyses
- Official defense and trade ministry publications
## 8. Approved public sources

- SIPRI
- IISS
- CSIS
- RAND
- Brookings
- World Bank
- IMF
- UN Comtrade
## 9. Source verification standards

- Great-power competition is not monolithic; note areas of cooperation (climate, nuclear non-proliferation).
Avoid deterministic 'Thucydides trap' framing; note its contested empirical basis. Distinguish capability from
intention.
## 10. Analytical framework

- Great-power-framework: map competition domains, analyze military/economic/technological dynamics, assess institutional competition, and forecast trajectories and escalation risks.
## 11. Evidence requirements

- Use the ANBU seven-label evidence vocabulary (FACT, VERIFIED, UNVERIFIED, ASSESSMENT, INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS,
SPECULATION). Every claim must carry an evidence label. Load-bearing claims require corroboration. Inferences
must be logically derivable from cited evidence. Speculation must be explicitly labeled and confined to
clearly marked sections.
## 12. Confidence assessment

- Assess confidence on three dimensions: source confidence (trustworthiness of origin), information confidence
(reliability of the specific item), and analytical confidence (soundness of the inference). Report all three.
Use HIGH (multiple corroborated sources, strong analytical basis), MODERATE (single reliable source or limited
corroboration), LOW (weak sourcing or significant analytical uncertainty), INSUFFICIENT (inadequate evidence
to support assessment).
## 13. Common analytical mistakes

- Treating competition as inevitable rather than contingent.
- Ignoring areas of cooperation and shared interests.
- Assuming military competition is the only domain.
- Overstating technological determinism.
## 14. Ethical considerations

- Analysis only; no assistance in great-power conflict escalation or targeting.
## 15. Security boundaries

- Public sources only. Do not facilitate attacks on great-power actors or assistance to armed groups.
## 16. Expected output

- A great-power brief: domain map, competition analysis, trajectory forecast, escalation risks, confidence
labels.
## 17. Quality checklist

- Dyads defined
- Domains mapped
- Competition analyzed
- Trajectory forecast
- Escalation risks assessed
- Confidence graded
