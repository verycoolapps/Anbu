# Border Dispute Public Analysis
**Division:** Geopolitical | **Type:** Analytical Reference | **Version:** 1.0
## 1. Purpose

- Analyze border and boundary disputes using only public sources — demarcation history, incident records, confidence-building measures, and third-party mediation.
## 2. Scope

- Boundary-level disputes: demarcation, border incidents, cross-border movement, and boundary-management mechanisms. Strategic territorial disputes are covered in territorial_dispute_analysis.md.
## 3. Intelligence objectives

- Reconstruct the boundary's legal-historical basis from public documents.
- Catalog border incidents and their frequency/severity patterns.
- Assess confidence-building measures and their effectiveness.
- Evaluate the dispute's impact on communities and bilateral relations.
## 4. Core concepts

- Boundary demarcation vs delimitation
- Incident patterns
- Confidence-building measures
- Border management regimes
## 5. Analytical methodology

- Boundary-assessment framework: establish the boundary's legal basis, catalog incidents by type and frequency, evaluate CBMs, and assess impact on communities and relations.
## 6. Step-by-step workflow

- Define the boundary segment and dispute scope.
- Collect boundary treaties, demarcation maps, and joint statements.
- Catalog incidents: crossings, firings, detentions, infrastructure disputes.
- Assess CBMs: hotlines, joint patrols, liaison mechanisms, trade crossings.
- Evaluate impact on border communities and bilateral relations.
- Produce the boundary analysis with confidence labels.
## 7. Data requirements

- Boundary treaties and protocols
- Joint boundary commission reports
- Official incident statements
- News reporting from border regions
- Think-tank boundary studies
- Academic borderlands scholarship
## 8. Approved public sources

- UN
- National MFAs and boundary commissions
- CSIS
- IISS
- Reuters/AP
- Academic borderlands studies
## 9. Source verification standards

- Incident reports often come from one side only — flag single-source claims. Distinguish deliberate incidents
from accidents or misunderstandings where evidence allows.
## 10. Analytical framework

- Boundary-assessment framework: establish the boundary's legal basis, catalog incidents by type and frequency, evaluate CBMs, and assess impact on communities and relations.
## 11. Evidence requirements

- Use the ANBU seven-label evidence vocabulary (FACT, VERIFIED, UNVERIFIED, ASSESSMENT, INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS,
SPECULATION). Every claim must carry an evidence label. Load-bearing claims require corroboration. Inferences
must be logically derivable from cited evidence. Speculation must be explicitly labeled and confined to
clearly marked sections.
## 12. Confidence assessment

- Assess confidence on three dimensions: source confidence (trustworthiness of origin), information confidence
(reliability of the specific item), and analytical confidence (soundness of the inference). Report all three.
Use HIGH (multiple corroborated sources, strong analytical basis), MODERATE (single reliable source or limited
corroboration), LOW (weak sourcing or significant analytical uncertainty), INSUFFICIENT (inadequate evidence
to support assessment).
## 13. Common analytical mistakes

- Counting incidents without normalizing for reporting incentives.
- Assuming silence means calm rather than unreported tension.
- Ignoring livelihood and community dimensions.
- Treating boundary disputes as purely military rather than socio-economic.
## 14. Ethical considerations

- Analysis only; no assistance in border infiltration or cross-border operations.
## 15. Security boundaries

- Public sources only. Do not facilitate illegal border crossing, smuggling, or targeting of border communities.
## 16. Expected output

- A boundary dossier: legal basis, incident catalog, CBM assessment, community impact, confidence labels.
## 17. Quality checklist

- Boundary defined
- Treaties documented
- Incidents cataloged
- CBMs assessed
- Community impact noted
- Confidence graded
