# Global Supply Chain Analysis
**Division:** Geopolitical | **Type:** Analytical Reference | **Version:** 1.0
## 1. Purpose

- Analyze global supply chains — semiconductor, pharmaceutical, automotive, and critical goods — for concentration risk, resilience, and geopolitical leverage.
## 2. Scope

- Cross-border production networks: where goods are made, assembled, and shipped — concentration, resilience, and strategic dependence. Single-commodity analysis is covered under critical_mineral_geopolitics.md.
## 3. Intelligence objectives

- Map global supply chains for key sectors and products.
- Identify concentration points and single points of failure.
- Assess resilience: diversification, redundancy, shock absorption.
- Analyze geopolitical leverage: who can disrupt, who depends.
## 4. Core concepts

- Supply-chain concentration
- Resilience and redundancy
- Geopolitical leverage
- Friend-shoring and decoupling
## 5. Analytical methodology

- Supply-chain-framework: map production networks, identify concentration and chokepoints, assess resilience mechanisms, and evaluate geopolitical leverage and policy responses.
## 6. Step-by-step workflow

- Define the sector or product scope.
- Map production networks: suppliers, manufacturers, assemblers, logistics.
- Identify concentration: geographic, corporate, and single-source risks.
- Assess resilience: inventory buffers, alternative suppliers, nearshoring.
- Analyze leverage: who can disrupt, who bears the cost?
- Evaluate policy responses: subsidies, reshoring, diversification.
## 7. Data requirements

- UN Comtrade and trade data
- Industry association reports
- Company disclosures and supply-chain maps
- Think-tank supply-chain studies (CSIS, RAND)
- Logistics and shipping data
- Academic operations-research literature
## 8. Approved public sources

- UN Comtrade
- Industry associations
- Company filings
- CSIS
- RAND
- Clarksons
- Academic literature
## 9. Source verification standards

- Supply-chain data is often proprietary; public data is partial. Distinguish direct suppliers from
tier-2/tier-3 dependencies. Note that 'resilience' is costly and context-specific.
## 10. Analytical framework

- Supply-chain-framework: map production networks, identify concentration and chokepoints, assess resilience mechanisms, and evaluate geopolitical leverage and policy responses.
## 11. Evidence requirements

- Use the ANBU seven-label evidence vocabulary (FACT, VERIFIED, UNVERIFIED, ASSESSMENT, INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS,
SPECULATION). Every claim must carry an evidence label. Load-bearing claims require corroboration. Inferences
must be logically derivable from cited evidence. Speculation must be explicitly labeled and confined to
clearly marked sections.
## 12. Confidence assessment

- Assess confidence on three dimensions: source confidence (trustworthiness of origin), information confidence
(reliability of the specific item), and analytical confidence (soundness of the inference). Report all three.
Use HIGH (multiple corroborated sources, strong analytical basis), MODERATE (single reliable source or limited
corroboration), LOW (weak sourcing or significant analytical uncertainty), INSUFFICIENT (inadequate evidence
to support assessment).
## 13. Common analytical mistakes

- Treating supply chains as linear rather than networked.
- Ignoring tier-2/tier-3 dependencies.
- Assuming diversification is always feasible.
- Missing the role of logistics and shipping in resilience.
## 14. Ethical considerations

- Analysis only; no assistance in supply-chain sabotage or corporate espionage.
## 15. Security boundaries

- Public sources only. Do not facilitate sanctions evasion, corporate espionage, or attacks on logistics.
## 16. Expected output

- A supply-chain brief: network map, concentration risks, resilience assessment, leverage analysis, policy
responses, confidence labels.
## 17. Quality checklist

- Scope defined
- Networks mapped
- Concentration assessed
- Resilience evaluated
- Leverage analyzed
- Confidence graded
