# Global Power Structure Analysis
**Division:** Geopolitical | **Type:** Analytical Reference | **Version:** 1.0
## 1. Purpose

- Characterize the systemic distribution of power — unipolar, bipolar, or multipolar — and its implications for alliance behavior, conflict risk, and institutional order.
## 2. Scope

- The system level: great-power distribution, institutional leadership, and the rules-based order's health. Regional detail is covered in regional files.
## 3. Intelligence objectives

- Characterize the current power structure and its trajectory.
- Assess the health of the institutional order (UN, Bretton Woods, alliances).
- Identify power-transition risks and their historical analogues.
- Evaluate implications for alliance cohesion and conflict probability.
## 4. Core concepts

- Power transition theory
- Unipolarity vs multipolarity
- Institutional order and legitimacy
- Hedging and bandwagoning
## 5. Analytical methodology

- Systemic-framework analysis: measure power distribution across dimensions, assess institutional order health, apply power-transition logic, and derive implications for state behavior and conflict risk.
## 6. Step-by-step workflow

- Establish the time horizon and the dimensions of power to measure.
- Collect systemic indicators: SIPRI spending, GDP shares, institutional leadership, alliance networks.
- Assess order health: UN functionality, treaty compliance, norms erosion.
- Apply power-transition analysis: is the rising power approaching parity? Where?
- Evaluate implications: alliance behavior, middle-power strategies, conflict risk.
- Deliver the systemic assessment with scenario branches.
## 7. Data requirements

- SIPRI military expenditure
- World Bank and IMF global data
- UN system performance records
- Alliance and treaty documentation
- Think-tank systemic analyses (IISS Military Balance, CSIS)
- Academic power-transition literature
## 8. Approved public sources

- SIPRI
- World Bank
- IMF
- UN
- IISS
- CSIS
- RAND
- Academic IR scholarship
## 9. Source verification standards

- Power-transition is probabilistic, not deterministic. Label historical analogies as analogical reasoning, not
evidence. Avoid monocausal explanations.
## 10. Analytical framework

- Systemic-framework analysis: measure power distribution across dimensions, assess institutional order health, apply power-transition logic, and derive implications for state behavior and conflict risk.
## 11. Evidence requirements

- Use the ANBU seven-label evidence vocabulary (FACT, VERIFIED, UNVERIFIED, ASSESSMENT, INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS,
SPECULATION). Every claim must carry an evidence label. Load-bearing claims require corroboration. Inferences
must be logically derivable from cited evidence. Speculation must be explicitly labeled and confined to
clearly marked sections.
## 12. Confidence assessment

- Assess confidence on three dimensions: source confidence (trustworthiness of origin), information confidence
(reliability of the specific item), and analytical confidence (soundness of the inference). Report all three.
Use HIGH (multiple corroborated sources, strong analytical basis), MODERATE (single reliable source or limited
corroboration), LOW (weak sourcing or significant analytical uncertainty), INSUFFICIENT (inadequate evidence
to support assessment).
## 13. Common analytical mistakes

- Declaring a 'new Cold War' without specifying structure.
- Ignoring economic interdependence as a constraint on conflict.
- Treating middle powers as passive objects rather than agents.
- Assuming institutional decline equals anarchy.
## 14. Ethical considerations

- Analysis only; no advocacy for or against any power configuration.
## 15. Security boundaries

- Public sources only. No assistance in power-projection strategy or alliance manipulation.
## 16. Expected output

- A systemic assessment: power-structure characterization, order-health grade, transition-risk analysis,
scenario implications.
## 17. Quality checklist

- Time horizon stated
- Power dimensions measured
- Order health assessed
- Transition risk analyzed probabilistically
- Implications for behavior derived
- Confidence graded
