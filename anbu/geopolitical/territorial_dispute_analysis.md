# Territorial Dispute Analysis
**Division:** Geopolitical | **Type:** Analytical Reference | **Version:** 1.0
## 1. Purpose

- Analyze territorial disputes — land, maritime, and aerial — for legal status, strategic stakes, escalation dynamics, and management mechanisms.
## 2. Scope

- Territorial disputes between states: land borders, maritime zones, islands, and airspace. Boundary demarcation details belong to border_dispute_public_analysis.md.
## 3. Intelligence objectives

- Establish the legal status and history of a territorial dispute.
- Assess strategic stakes: resources, symbolism, domestic politics.
- Map claimant positions, management mechanisms, and flashpoints.
- Evaluate escalation risk and conflict-management options.
## 4. Core concepts

- Territorial integrity norm
- Competing sovereignty claims
- Strategic vs symbolic stakes
- Dispute-management mechanisms
## 5. Analytical methodology

- Dispute-analysis framework: establish the legal-historical basis of claims, assess material and symbolic stakes, map claimant positions and management mechanisms, and evaluate escalation dynamics.
## 6. Step-by-step workflow

- Identify the dispute and all claimants; establish the legal basis of each claim.
- Review historical and treaty context; note ICJ/ITLOS involvement.
- Assess stakes: resources, strategic geography, domestic politics, symbolism.
- Map management mechanisms: joint commissions, confidence-building, hotline.
- Evaluate flashpoints and escalation history.
- Produce the dispute assessment with confidence labels.
## 7. Data requirements

- UNCLOS and treaty texts
- ICJ and ITLOS judgments
- Official maps and notes verbales
- Think-tank dispute studies (CSIS, IISS)
- News reporting on incidents
- Academic international-law scholarship
## 8. Approved public sources

- UN (ICJ, ITLOS)
- UNCLOS
- National MFAs
- CSIS
- IISS
- Academic legal scholarship
## 9. Source verification standards

- Legal claims and political claims differ; present both. Do not adjudicate — analyze. Note that maps themselves
are political documents; cite the issuing authority.
## 10. Analytical framework

- Dispute-analysis framework: establish the legal-historical basis of claims, assess material and symbolic stakes, map claimant positions and management mechanisms, and evaluate escalation dynamics.
## 11. Evidence requirements

- Use the ANBU seven-label evidence vocabulary (FACT, VERIFIED, UNVERIFIED, ASSESSMENT, INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS,
SPECULATION). Every claim must carry an evidence label. Load-bearing claims require corroboration. Inferences
must be logically derivable from cited evidence. Speculation must be explicitly labeled and confined to
clearly marked sections.
## 12. Confidence assessment

- Assess confidence on three dimensions: source confidence (trustworthiness of origin), information confidence
(reliability of the specific item), and analytical confidence (soundness of the inference). Report all three.
Use HIGH (multiple corroborated sources, strong analytical basis), MODERATE (single reliable source or limited
corroboration), LOW (weak sourcing or significant analytical uncertainty), INSUFFICIENT (inadequate evidence
to support assessment).
## 13. Common analytical mistakes

- Treating de facto control as de jure title.
- Ignoring domestic politics that constrain compromise.
- Assuming legal clarity where claims are genuinely ambiguous.
- Focusing on state claims while ignoring sub-state actors.
## 14. Ethical considerations

- Analysis only; no assistance in strengthening claims by force or fabricating evidence.
## 15. Security boundaries

- Public sources only. Do not fabricate historical documents or maps; do not assist in seizing territory.
## 16. Expected output

- A dispute dossier: legal basis, stakes, claimant positions, management mechanisms, escalation assessment,
confidence.
## 17. Quality checklist

- Dispute defined
- Legal basis documented
- Stakes assessed
- Management mechanisms mapped
- Escalation risk graded
- Confidence stated
