# Food Security Geopolitics Analysis
**Division:** Geopolitical | **Type:** Analytical Reference | **Version:** 1.0
## 1. Purpose

- Analyze the geopolitical dimensions of food security: production geography, trade dependence, price volatility, export restrictions, and food as an instrument of statecraft.
## 2. Scope

- Global and regional food systems: grain, fertilizer, and agricultural inputs — production, trade, prices, and strategic leverage. Nutrition detail is out of scope.
## 3. Intelligence objectives

- Map global food production, trade flows, and import dependence.
- Analyze price volatility drivers and food-price transmission to unrest.
- Assess export restrictions and food weaponization history.
- Evaluate strategic reserves and food self-sufficiency policies.
## 4. Core concepts

- Food as strategic commodity
- Export restrictions and weaponization
- Import dependence vulnerability
- Fertilizer and input geopolitics
## 5. Analytical methodology

- Food-security framework: map production/trade/consumption, assess import dependence and vulnerability, analyze price dynamics and unrest links, evaluate weaponization history and reserves.
## 6. Step-by-step workflow

- Define the geographic and commodity scope.
- Collect production, trade, and stock data from FAO and USDA.
- Map import dependence ratios for key commodities.
- Analyze price volatility: futures, spot, transmission to consumer prices.
- Review export-restriction history and strategic reserves.
- Assess unrest correlations and strategic implications.
## 7. Data requirements

- FAO and USDA data
- World Bank food-price indices
- UN Comtrade agricultural trade
- IEA fertilizer and energy data
- Think-tank food-security studies (IFPRI, CSIS)
- Official agriculture ministry data
## 8. Approved public sources

- FAO
- USDA
- World Bank
- UN Comtrade
- IFPRI
- CSIS
- National agriculture ministries
## 9. Source verification standards

- Food-security statistics have significant revision lags; use latest available and note vintage. Distinguish
caloric availability from nutritional adequacy. Price data varies by market.
## 10. Analytical framework

- Food-security framework: map production/trade/consumption, assess import dependence and vulnerability, analyze price dynamics and unrest links, evaluate weaponization history and reserves.
## 11. Evidence requirements

- Use the ANBU seven-label evidence vocabulary (FACT, VERIFIED, UNVERIFIED, ASSESSMENT, INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS,
SPECULATION). Every claim must carry an evidence label. Load-bearing claims require corroboration. Inferences
must be logically derivable from cited evidence. Speculation must be explicitly labeled and confined to
clearly marked sections.
## 12. Confidence assessment

- Assess confidence on three dimensions: source confidence (trustworthiness of origin), information confidence
(reliability of the specific item), and analytical confidence (soundness of the inference). Report all three.
Use HIGH (multiple corroborated sources, strong analytical basis), MODERATE (single reliable source or limited
corroboration), LOW (weak sourcing or significant analytical uncertainty), INSUFFICIENT (inadequate evidence
to support assessment).
## 13. Common analytical mistakes

- Treating production data as consumption data.
- Ignoring input dependencies (fertilizer, energy, water).
- Assuming self-sufficiency is achievable or desirable for all states.
- Missing sub-national and household-level food insecurity.
## 14. Ethical considerations

- Analysis only; no assistance in food sanctions evasion or agricultural sabotage.
## 15. Security boundaries

- Public sources only. Do not facilitate food aid diversion, sanctions evasion, or attacks on agricultural
infrastructure.
## 16. Expected output

- A food-security brief: production/trade map, dependence assessment, price analysis, weaponization history,
confidence labels.
## 17. Quality checklist

- Scope defined
- Production/trade mapped
- Dependence assessed
- Price dynamics analyzed
- Weaponization reviewed
- Confidence graded
