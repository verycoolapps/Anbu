# Trade Route Analysis
**Division:** Geopolitical | **Type:** Analytical Reference | **Version:** 1.0
## 1. Purpose

- Analyze global trade routes — maritime chokepoints, overland corridors, and pipeline networks — for vulnerability, redundancy, and strategic significance.
## 2. Scope

- Physical trade corridors: sea lanes, chokepoints, rail/road corridors, and pipelines. Trade policy and tariffs are covered under international_trade_policy.md.
## 3. Intelligence objectives

- Map critical trade routes and their dependence on chokepoints.
- Assess vulnerability: geopolitical, natural, and criminal risks.
- Evaluate redundancy and alternative routes.
- Analyze strategic significance: who benefits, who is exposed.
## 4. Core concepts

- Trade chokepoints
- Route redundancy
- Supply-chain resilience
- Strategic corridor competition
## 5. Analytical methodology

- Trade-route framework: map routes and volumes, identify chokepoints and dependence, assess vulnerabilities and alternatives, and evaluate strategic implications for states and blocs.
## 6. Step-by-step workflow

- Define the trade scope (commodity, region, or global).
- Collect trade flow data from UN Comtrade and logistics sources.
- Map routes: maritime lanes, chokepoints, overland corridors, pipelines.
- Assess dependence: what share of trade depends on each chokepoint?
- Evaluate vulnerability: geopolitical risk, piracy, natural hazards.
- Identify alternatives and their capacity/cost.
## 7. Data requirements

- UN Comtrade trade data
- Clarksons and shipping data
- Official port and logistics statistics
- World Bank trade logistics index
- Think-tank trade-route studies (CSIS, RAND)
- Satellite and AIS tracking data
## 8. Approved public sources

- UN Comtrade
- Clarksons
- World Bank
- Clarksons Research
- CSIS
- RAND
- Port authorities
## 9. Source verification standards

- Trade data has reporting lags and mirror-statistics discrepancies; note both. Distinguish capacity from actual
usage. AIS data has gaps (dark vessels).
## 10. Analytical framework

- Trade-route framework: map routes and volumes, identify chokepoints and dependence, assess vulnerabilities and alternatives, and evaluate strategic implications for states and blocs.
## 11. Evidence requirements

- Use the ANBU seven-label evidence vocabulary (FACT, VERIFIED, UNVERIFIED, ASSESSMENT, INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS,
SPECULATION). Every claim must carry an evidence label. Load-bearing claims require corroboration. Inferences
must be logically derivable from cited evidence. Speculation must be explicitly labeled and confined to
clearly marked sections.
## 12. Confidence assessment

- Assess confidence on three dimensions: source confidence (trustworthiness of origin), information confidence
(reliability of the specific item), and analytical confidence (soundness of the inference). Report all three.
Use HIGH (multiple corroborated sources, strong analytical basis), MODERATE (single reliable source or limited
corroboration), LOW (weak sourcing or significant analytical uncertainty), INSUFFICIENT (inadequate evidence
to support assessment).
## 13. Common analytical mistakes

- Treating route capacity as route usage.
- Ignoring overland alternatives that are costlier but viable.
- Assuming chokepoint closure is binary rather than degraded.
- Missing the role of non-state actors (piracy, sanctions evasion).
## 14. Ethical considerations

- Analysis only; no assistance in disrupting trade routes or sanctions evasion.
## 15. Security boundaries

- Public sources only. Do not facilitate piracy, sanctions evasion, or attacks on trade infrastructure.
## 16. Expected output

- A trade-route brief: route map, chokepoint analysis, vulnerability assessment, alternatives evaluation,
confidence labels.
## 17. Quality checklist

- Scope defined
- Routes mapped
- Chokepoints identified
- Dependence quantified
- Vulnerabilities assessed
- Confidence graded
